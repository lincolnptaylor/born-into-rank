Key Terms

Class
A group based on money, education, and jobs. Unlike caste, people can sometimes move up or down.
Varna
One of the four ranked groups described in the Rigveda. They are the Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras.
Dalit
The name used today for people once called "untouchables," who were ranked below all four Varnas.
Untouchability
The practice of treating Dalits as unclean, so higher castes would not touch them or eat with them. India banned it in 1950.
Overview

Abstract

For this project, I wanted to answer two questions. Does caste still matter today? And does America have a caste system like India's? I argue that caste, which started in India over 3,000 years ago, still affects who gets money, jobs, and respect. I use three pieces of evidence to prove it. The first is the Rigveda, an ancient Hindu text that split society into four ranked groups. The second is India today, where caste was banned in 1950 but still affects marriage, school, and jobs. The third is Isabel Wilkerson's book Caste, which shows that America has a caste system too. My evidence shows that caste still matters, and that India and America both have caste systems, even though they work in different ways.

Background

Introduction

Over 3,000 years ago, an ancient Hindu text ranked people into groups by birth. That ranking system, called caste, has lasted longer than almost any other way of ranking people. Its age was the most surprising discovery of my research.

A caste is a discriminatory ranking system that people are born into, based on family, color, and race. No one can leave their caste, and it determines a person's job, whom they may marry, and how others treat them.

Caste continues to harm people, and America has a caste system as well. India outlawed caste discrimination in 1950, but the law did not end it. A problem cannot be solved until it is recognized.

The historian's tool

What is Argument + Evidence?

I chose Argument + Evidence because it suits my topic and is a tool I use well.

An opinion is a personal preference. Saying "I like cats" is an opinion. An argument is a claim that someone tries to prove, and saying "cats are better than dogs" is an argument. An argument can be made with or without evidence, but a historian must support every argument with evidence.

Strong evidence comes from a trustworthy source. A claim that cats are easier to care for, supported simply by asking an AI chatbot, could easily be false. Good evidence must also connect directly to the claim. The historian then explains how each piece of evidence supports the argument.

Supporting the argument

My Evidence

Evidence 1

How caste started in India

Over 3,000 years ago, an ancient Hindu text called the Rigveda (hymn 10.90) described society as four ranked groups called Varnas.

RankVarnaRole
1BrahminsPriests and teachers
2KshatriyasRulers and warriors
3VaishyasMerchants and farmers
4ShudrasWorkers and servants

People outside the system, later called "untouchables" and today called Dalits, were ranked lowest of all. Over time this split into thousands of smaller groups tied to family jobs. People were required to marry within their group and to do their family's work, and higher castes would not eat with or touch lower castes.

Why it supports my argument

In a class system, people can sometimes improve their position through work. In the caste system, they could not, no matter how hard they worked. A person's rank, along with their wealth or poverty, passed down to their children. In this way, wealth and poverty became something families inherit.

SourcesThe Rigveda, hymn 10.90, translated by H. H. Wilson (Wisdom Library); Isabel Wilkerson, Caste: The Origins of Our Discontents

Evidence 2

Caste still matters in India today

In 1950, India's new Constitution banned untouchability (Article 17). The team that wrote it was led by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, who was born a Dalit himself. India also saved spots in colleges and government jobs for lower castes, and those spots still exist today because the gaps never fully closed.

1 in 20

Only about 5 percent of Indians say they are married to someone from a different caste (Ahuja, 2016).

Why it supports my argument

Seventy-five years after it was outlawed, caste still affects whom people marry and who gains access to good schools and jobs. That shows caste still shapes class today.

SourcesConstitution of India, Article 17 (1950); Amit Ahuja, "Caste and Marriage in Urban Middle-Class India," Center for the Advanced Study of India, University of Pennsylvania (2016)

Evidence 3

Caste in America

In her book Caste (2020), Isabel Wilkerson argues that America has its own caste system, based on race instead of religion. She compares America, India, and Nazi Germany and finds eight pillars all three share, such as ranking people by birth, controlling who can marry whom, and keeping certain groups out of certain jobs.

In 1959, a school principal in India introduced Martin Luther King Jr. to Dalit students as a fellow untouchable from America.

King was surprised at first, but he soon recognized the truth of the comparison because of how Black Americans were treated under segregation. This story shows that caste crosses borders.

My take

I agree with Wilkerson. India and America both have caste systems, but they operate differently. India's system was formally organized, while America's has been less structured and more informal.

Why it supports my argument

Wilkerson shows that the pattern of ranking people at birth appears in other countries, including the United States. In 2023, Seattle became the first U.S. city to ban caste discrimination, which shows that it still occurs here.

SourcesIsabel Wilkerson, Caste: The Origins of Our Discontents (2020); "Seattle Becomes the First U.S. City to Ban Caste Discrimination," NPR (2023)

Comparing caste in India and America

India

India's caste system grew out of religion and was written down in the Rigveda, with four Varnas and thousands of smaller groups. It was banned in 1950, but it still affects who people marry and what jobs they get.

America

America's caste system is based on race rather than religion, and it is less formally organized than India's. Laws and customs such as segregation kept it in place, and it continues to shape who gets ahead.

Final thoughts

Conclusion

My research answered both of my questions. Caste still matters today, and America does have a caste system, although it works differently from India's. India's system was formally organized around religion, while America's is less structured and based on race.

Together, my three pieces of evidence support my argument. The Rigveda shows where ranking by birth started over 3,000 years ago. India today shows that even after caste was banned in 1950, it still affects whom people marry and who gains access to good schools and jobs. Isabel Wilkerson and the Martin Luther King Jr. story show that caste crosses borders. Seattle's 2023 ban shows that caste discrimination exists in America today. People are working against it, but much remains to be done.

The most important lesson of this project is that humanity never learns from history. A ranking system from over 3,000 years ago still harms people. I hope this website encourages readers to learn more about caste.
